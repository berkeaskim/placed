# 05 — AI-written cover letters: recruiter attitudes, detection, and keeping the candidate's voice

Researcher angle: how recruiters view AI-written applications (2023-2026), what the evidence says about whether AI help works, which textual tells give AI away, and how Placed's writer can keep a letter in the student's own voice while staying honest.

Researched 2026-10-17. Survey figures from vendors (Resume Genius/CV Genius, TopResume, Greenhouse) are commercially motivated, so treat them as directional only.

---

## 1. Key findings

### F1. AI help with *polish* gets people hired; AI that *replaces effort* destroys the signal. **STRONG**
- In a field experiment with nearly half a million jobseekers, non-generative writing help (fixing errors, improving readability) got treated jobseekers hired about 8% more often at about 10% higher wages, and employers were no less satisfied ([Wiles, Munyikwa & Horton, Management Science 2025 / NBER w30886](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30886)). The authors' mechanism: clearer writing helps employers see real ability. It does not *fake* ability.
- When Freelancer.com launched a one-click AI cover-letter tool, letters matched job posts more closely and callbacks rose. But the link between how well a letter matched the post and getting a callback fell by about 51% (and by about 79% for offers), and employers shifted to other signals such as work history and reviews ([Cui, Dias & Ye 2025, arXiv 2509.25054](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25054)).
- In the same data, **more time spent editing the AI draft went with more offers**. Over 75% of AI letters were submitted within a minute.
- Before LLMs, employers paid a premium for customised proposals. After LLMs they did not, and AI-written proposals no longer predicted job completion. The authors' simulated market becomes "significantly less meritocratic": the strongest workers are hired 19% less often ([Galdin & Silbert, "Making Talk Cheap", arXiv 2511.08785](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08785)).
- **Implication for Placed:** a generic tailored-sounding letter is now worthless as a signal. The value has to come from content that is verifiable, specific to the person, and costly to fake. That means real CV facts, real anecdotes and a real reason for wanting the role, with the user editing the draft.

### F2. Recruiters are suspicious of AI-sounding letters and penalise them, though outright rejection is a minority view. **MODERATE**
- A 2024 survey of 625 hiring managers (reported as both [Resume Genius, US](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/04/08/3-resume-red-flags-recruiters-look-out-for-and-how-to-avoid-them.html) and [CV Genius, UK](https://www.digit.fyi/80-of-hiring-managers-critical-of-ai-generated-content/)) found about 80% distrust AI-generated content, and 53-57% are less likely to hire a candidate they suspect used AI. They describe such applications as "repetitive, generic, and lazy" ([Black Enterprise summary](https://blackenterprise.com/hiring-managers-reject-ai-generated-job-applications)).
- TopResume (about 600 US managers) found a narrower effect. Nearly 20% would reject a candidate over an AI-generated CV or cover letter, and 52% accept AI for proofreading or drafting *if the result reads as human* ([TopResume](https://topresume.com/career-advice/ai-in-hiring-survey)).
- In experiments, simply *suspecting* AI lowered ratings of writing quality and hiring intent "across the board" ([Kadoma et al., "Perceptual harms", arXiv 2410.00906](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.00906)). When readers attributed a college personal statement to AI, they rated it lower on quality and authenticity and rated the applicant lower on competence and morality ([arXiv 2412.18647](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.18647)).

### F3. UK graduate employers say: AI is OK as a starting point, but it must be authentic, and you must be able to defend it. **MODERATE**
- ISE (Institute of Student Employers): 140 applications per graduate vacancy in 2023/24, up 59%. Joint CEO Stephen Isherwood says AI pushes volume up and quality down, and that AI-enhanced applications "must be authentic". 43% of employers have no problem with AI use at some stages, and 32% advise against it but take no action ([Personnel Today / ISE coverage](https://www.personneltoday.com/?p=313212); [Prospects Luminate](https://luminate.prospects.ac.uk/three-key-trends-in-graduate-recruitment-this-year); [ISE 2025 survey](https://ise.org.uk/_userfiles/pages/files/reports/student_recruitment_survey_2025.pdf)). Later ISE reporting says 92% of employers had encountered or suspected AI misconduct ([HR Grapevine](https://www.hrgrapevine.com/content/article/ise-2025-10-21-whats-really-happening-in-graduate-recruitment-ais-role)).
- UK law firms ([LawCareers.Net 2026](https://www.lawcareers.net/Explore/Oracle/13012026-Using-AI-in-law-firm-applications); [Legal Cheek on Shoosmiths](https://www.legalcheek.com/2024/02/shoosmiths-advises-tc-seekers-on-ai-usage-in-applications/)):
  - **HFW:** accepts AI "as a starting point", provided the candidate understands what they submit.
  - **Trowers & Hamlins:** AI cannot replicate personal experience.
  - **Clifford Chance:** "overreliance may weaken your application".
  - **Shoosmiths:** wants the candidate's "unique voice" and refuses copy-paste answers.
  - **A&O Shearman:** be ready to explain your AI use at interview.
  - **Taylor Wessing:** AI is fine for drafting cover letters but not for online assessments.
  - **12 King's Bench Walk (barristers' chambers):** AI-generated text counts as cheating even if edited.
- Some employers ban AI outright. For example, Anthropic asks applicants not to use AI assistants so it can assess their unassisted communication and genuine interest ([Fortune, Feb 2025](https://fortune.com/2025/02/04/anthropic-tells-job-candidates-dont-use-ai-employer-trend)).
- Bright Network: 38% of 14,000+ students had used generative AI in applications (2024), rising to 58% later. Its advice is that a letter "anyone could have written for any job" fails ([Bright Network guide](https://brightnetwork.co.uk/graduate-career-advice/complete-guide-ai-for-job-applications)).

### F4. Volume is exploding, so generic letters drown. **MODERATE**
- Greenhouse: applications per job rose 102% after ChatGPT (Q3 2022 vs Q4 2024). Its CEO calls the market an "AI doom loop" ([Greenhouse 2025 AI in Hiring report](https://www.greenhouse.com/uk/newsroom/an-ai-trust-crisis-70-of-hiring-managers-trust-ai-to-make-faster-and-better-hiring-decisions-only-8-of-job-seekers-call-it-fair); [Fortune Nov 2025](https://fortune.com/2025/11/18/hiring-job-seekers-recruiters-talent-acquisition-ai-doom-loop-application-technology)). Vendor data.

### F5. People are bad at detecting AI text, but they rely on surface cues, and those cues hurt you if present. **STRONG**
- Across 6 experiments with 4,600 participants, people could not tell AI-written self-presentations (including professional profiles) from human ones, scoring around chance. Their heuristics were flawed: they treated first-person pronouns, contractions and family topics as "human" ([Jakesch, Hancock & Naaman, PNAS 2023](https://sml.stanford.edu/publications/2023/human-heuristics-ai-generated-language-are-flawed)).
- Faculty identified AI versus human residency personal statements only 59% of the time ([CMEJ 2025, PMC12826822](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12826822/)). Surgical reviewers also could not reliably tell ([Surgery 2024, PMID 39299851](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39299851/)).
- Detectors are unreliable and biased. Seven GPT detectors misclassified non-native TOEFL essays as AI at about 61% on average ([Liang et al., Patterns 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02819)). Readers also suspect international students of AI use more often ([arXiv 2412.18647](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.18647)).
- **Implication:** the goal is not to "evade detectors". It is to avoid the specific cues readers *use* (F6) and to be specific enough that the letter could only belong to this candidate. Do not build or advertise detector-beating. Never run a detector score as a quality gate, since it would penalise non-native students.

### F6. LLM text has measurable tells: vocabulary, rhetoric, uniform rhythm. **STRONG** (vocabulary and stylometry) / **MODERATE** (specific rhetorical patterns)
- **Vocabulary.** Kobak et al. analysed more than 15 million PubMed abstracts and found "excess" style words after ChatGPT, led by "delves" (roughly 25x the expected rate), plus "showcasing" and "underscores" (about 9x). At least 13.5% of 2024 abstracts were LLM-processed ([Kobak et al., Science Advances 2025 / arXiv 2406.07016](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07016)).
- **Community catalogue.** [Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing) (WikiProject AI Cleanup) lists:
  - **AI vocabulary:** delve, tapestry, testament, underscore, pivotal, crucial, landscape, vibrant, robust, meticulous, intricate, showcase, foster, garner, interplay, enduring, leverage, multifaceted, "align with", "deep dive".
  - **Copula avoidance:** "serves as", "stands as".
  - **Negative parallelisms:** "not just X but Y", "it's not X, it's Y".
  - **Rhetoric and form:** the rule of three, em-dash overuse, trailing "-ing" analyses ("highlighting...", "ensuring..."), promotional language, and vague attributions.
  - The page itself warns these are descriptive cues, not proof.
- **Grammar and rhetoric.** Instruction-tuned LLMs differ systematically from humans on Biber's grammatical and rhetorical features, leaning dense, nominal and academic ([Reinhart et al., PNAS 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.16107)). LLM argumentative text uses tricolon (groups of three) at nearly twice the expert rate, with "performed hesitancy" markers at double density and rhetorical devices spread unnaturally evenly ([arXiv 2604.19768](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.19768)).
- **Rhythm.** Human text has more scattered sentence lengths and more varied vocabulary. LLM output reads more uniform ([Muñoz-Ortiz et al., arXiv 2308.09067](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.09067)). AI text is "more formal, structured, positive, and motivational" ([iScience 2026, PMC12969083](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12969083/)). Lexical richness and entropy are the most robust signals across models and domains ([arXiv 2606.04177](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.04177); [arXiv 2608.27855](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27855)).
- **Generation vs editing.** AI-*edited* human text does not carry the same stylometric footprint as AI-*generated* text ([arXiv 2608.27855](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27855)). This strongly supports building the letter from the user's own words rather than from scratch.

### F7. LLM rewriting flattens personal voice, even when told to preserve it. **MODERATE**
- Across 300 personal narratives rewritten by three frontier LLMs, rewriting consistently:
  - removed function words, contractions and first-person pronouns;
  - raised vocabulary diversity, word length and punctuation complexity;
  - shifted narration from "embedded to distanced" and replaced causal reasoning with abstraction.
- "Voice-preserving" prompts reduced the effect but did not reverse its direction ([Voice Under Revision, arXiv 2604.22142](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22142)).
- AI suggestions pushed Indian writers toward Western styles, flattening cultural nuance ([Agarwal, Naaman & Vashistha, CHI 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.11360)). This matters for Placed's many international UCL students.
- **Implication:** constraints alone are not enough. Lint after generating, and keep the user's own sentences verbatim where possible.

### F8. Disclosing AI use costs trust; being caught hiding it costs more. **MODERATE**
- Across 13 experiments, disclosing AI use consistently lowered trust, even among frequent AI users. Concealed use that is later uncovered triggers the steepest drop ([Schilke & Reimann, OBHDP 2025](https://news.arizona.edu/news/disclosing-ai-use-can-backfire-research-shows)).
- **Implication:** the way out is not hiding AI use. It is making sure the letter is substantively the candidate's own, so they can honestly say "AI helped me edit" and defend every line at interview, as A&O Shearman and HFW expect.

### F9. AI drafts can teach good letter structure. **WEAK-MODERATE**
- In pre-registered experiments, people who practised cover letters with an AI tool later wrote *better letters without AI* ([Coach not crutch, arXiv 2502.02880](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02880)). GPT-4 cover letters were rated non-inferior to human ones in a small randomised blinded study ([PMID 38018708](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38018708/)).
- This supports an "explain the edits" mode in Placed.

## 2. Banned / flagged phrase list (regex lint)

Matching should be case-insensitive and on word boundaries.

- **HARD** = block or force a regeneration.
- **SOFT** = count it, warn when the density threshold is exceeded, and show it to the user.

Thresholds are per letter (about 250-400 words).

### D. Rhetorical structures (regex plus counters)
| Check | Severity |
|---|---|
| Negative parallelism `\bnot (only|just|merely)\b[^.]{1,80}\bbut( also)?\b` | SOFT (max 1) |
| `\bit'?s not\b[^.]{1,60}\bit'?s\b`, `\bnot because\b[^.]{1,60}\bbut because\b` | HARD |
| Em dash `—` (also ` -- ` and ` – ` used as an em dash) | SOFT: max 1 per letter; HARD when 3 or more |
| Triplets: `\b\w+, \w+,? and \w+\b` adjective/noun lists | SOFT: max 1 per letter |
| Trailing participle analysis `, (highlighting|underscoring|showcasing|reflecting|ensuring|demonstrating|fostering|contributing to)\b` | HARD |
| Copula avoidance `\b(serves|stands|acts) as\b` | SOFT |
| Rhetorical question opener `^[^.]{0,80}\?` in the first sentence | SOFT |
| Paragraph symmetry: coefficient of variation of paragraph word counts < 0.15 with 4 or more paragraphs | SOFT |
| Sentence-length uniformity: std-dev of sentence lengths < 4 words, or no sentence ≤ 8 words | SOFT |
| Exclamation marks `!` | HARD (none) |
| Markdown artifacts `\*\*`, `^#+ `, `^[-*] ` bullets, curly-quote/straight-quote mixing | HARD |
| Placeholders `\[(Company|Name|Hiring Manager|Your Name)[^\]]*\]`, `\{\{`, `<insert` | HARD |
| Chatbot leakage `(as an AI|I hope this (letter|helps)|here is (a|your) (draft|cover letter)|certainly!)` | HARD |
| American spelling in a UK letter `\b(organiz|optimiz|analyz|favor|center|program(?!me))` | SOFT (UK English) |

**Density rule (SOFT bucket):** more than 3 distinct SOFT hits per 300 words means regenerate the offending sentences. Do not just swap synonyms: replacing "delve" with "dig into" keeps the cadence.

## 3. Generation techniques that keep it human (prompt design)

1. **Facts first, prose second.** Build a structured "evidence pack" before any prose: 3-5 CV bullets that map to JD requirements, each with its source line ID. The prose model may only make claims that cite an evidence-pack item. This is the most important mechanism, because the economics papers (F1) show specificity and real effort are what still carry signal.
2. **Ask before writing (1-3 questions, max).** Generic letters come from missing inputs. Ask:
   - (a) "Why this firm, in one sentence: something you saw, read, or someone you met?"
   - (b) "Pick one experience from your CV and tell me what *you* did and what happened, in your own words."
   - (c) Optional: "Anything a recruiter wouldn't know from your CV?" (career switch, caring duties, a project).
   - Skippable. If skipped, the letter must not invent a motivation. Use the fallback in §4.
3. **Keep the user's phrasing verbatim.** Splice their answer sentences into the letter, fixing only spelling and grammar. Mark them as `locked` spans the model cannot rephrase. This fights the voice normalisation in F7: generation is stylometrically louder than editing.
4. **Capture a voice sample.** If the user has any of their own writing (a previous letter, their answers above, LinkedIn About), extract simple style stats from it: contraction use, average sentence length, first-person rate, formality. Pass those as targets, for example "use contractions like the sample (about 1 per 60 words)". Contractions and "I" are cues readers read as human (Jakesch), and LLM rewriting strips them (F7).
5. **One concrete scene beats three adjectives.** Require at least one sentence with a specific detail: a number, a named tool, module, client type, place or date from the CV. Replace every trait word ("analytical", "driven") with the evidence for it.
6. **Company specifics must be sourced.** Mention at most 1-2 company facts, each from the JD or a stored company note (deal, product, office, programme name). Never use generic praise. If there is no company fact, say why the *role* fits instead.
7. **Vary the rhythm on purpose.** Instruct: "mix short sentences (5-9 words) with longer ones; paragraphs of unequal length; no three-item lists unless the CV has exactly three things." Then verify with the counters in §2D.
8. **UK register.** British spelling, "Dear Hiring Manager" or a named person, "Yours sincerely" or "Kind regards", 250-400 words, no headings or bullets.
9. **Two-pass pipeline.**
   - (i) Draft from the evidence pack and the user's answers.
   - (ii) Run the lint (§2).
   - (iii) Targeted rewrite of only the flagged sentences, with the instruction "say the plain thing; use the user's words".
   - (iv) Re-lint.
   - (v) Show the user the remaining SOFT flags.
   - Cap at 2 regeneration rounds.
10. **Low temperature for facts, light variety for phrasing.** Avoid the "safe" high-probability phrasing that produces the clichés. Ban the opening clichés explicitly in the system prompt with a short negative list. Do not paste the whole list: long negative lists can prime the very words they ban.
11. **Prompt the user to edit.** Editing time predicted offers (F1). Show the letter with highlighted "your words" and "AI words", and nudge the user to personalise at least one sentence before export.
12. **Coach mode.** Optionally explain *why* each change was made (F9), so the student learns and can defend the letter at interview.

## 4. Honesty / integrity guardrails

1. **No invented experience.** Every claim about the candidate must trace to a CV line, a stored profile field, or a user answer. If the JD asks for something the user lacks, either omit it or frame it honestly (for example "I haven't used X professionally, but I used Y in my dissertation"). Never imply it.
2. **No invented numbers.** Percentages, counts, money and team sizes must appear verbatim in the source. No rounding up and no "approximately" invented figures.
3. **No invented motivations or relationships.** Do not invent "I met your team at...", "I've long admired...", family stories or personal hardship. If the user gave no motivation, use a factual role-fit sentence and flag "add your own reason". This matters because "why us" is precisely what employers who ban AI want to see as unassisted (Anthropic, F3).
4. **No invented company facts.** Company claims must come from the JD or stored research, with the source kept.
5. **Respect employer AI policies.** If the JD or company page says no AI (detect phrases like `do not use AI`, `without (the use of )?AI`, `AI-generated .* (not permitted|prohibited)`), warn the user. Offer "feedback only" mode (critique the user's own draft, with no generated text) instead of generation.
6. **Interview-defensible.** The user must be able to explain every sentence. Show a "you may be asked about this" note next to each claim (A&O Shearman, HFW expectations).
7. **No detector evasion features.** Don't market or optimise against AI detectors. Quality and specificity are the goal. Never score users with a detector: they are biased against non-native writers (Liang).
8. **No impersonation of a referee or another person**, and no fabricated quotes.

## 6. Open questions

1. **Thresholds are unvalidated.** The SOFT densities, em-dash limits and sentence-length std-dev are informed guesses. Calibrate them on a corpus of real UCL student letters versus raw LLM drafts. The lint should flag less than 10% of human letters.
2. **Negative-list priming.** Does listing banned words in the prompt raise their use? Lint-then-rewrite may be safer than prompt bans. This needs an A/B test on the models Placed supports (Ollama, Claude, Gemini).
3. **Model drift.** Tells change over time. The Wikipedia guide already marks em-dash overuse as a dated sign (2022 to September 2026), and newer models have new tics. The lint list should be data, not code, so it can be updated.
4. **Recruiter-side evidence for UK graduates specifically.** Most surveys are US vendor polls. No ISE data on penalties for AI-sounding letters was found, and no Big Four or MBB public guidance on candidate AI use was found.
5. **Disclosure.** Should Placed suggest a disclosure line? The evidence (Schilke & Reimann) says disclosure lowers trust, but being found concealing AI is worse. Probably the answer is no disclosure in the letter, plus readiness to explain at interview. Confirm this with careers advisers.
6. **Non-native students.** How do we preserve a non-native user's voice without leaving errors in? Wiles et al. show that fixing errors helps, while Agarwal et al. show AI erases cultural style. Where is the line?
7. **Employers that use AI scoring.** Do ATS or LLM screeners *prefer* AI-style prose? This is unknown, and it could conflict with the human-reader rules.
